## The Main Character

Frank Lloyd Wright, the iconic American architect, has long been celebrated for his visionary approach to design. But a new essay published in Aeon Magazine sheds light on an often overlooked aspect of his legacy - the way his work mirrored and shaped the American condition. 

The essay, written by architecture critic Paul Goldberger, argues that Wright's buildings were not just functional structures, but embodiments of the architect's deep-seated beliefs about individuality, democracy, and the relationship between humans and their environment. From the sprawling expanses of Fallingwater to the soaring, organic forms of the Guggenheim Museum, Wright's designs were a reflection of his conviction that the house should be a "work of art" - a canvas upon which the inhabitants could express their unique identities and values.

Goldberger traces Wright's influence from the early 20th century, when his Prairie-style homes challenged the stifling conformity of the Victorian era, to the present day, where his emphasis on open floor plans and seamless integration with nature continues to shape modern architecture. By understanding Wright's buildings as expressions of his philosophical worldview, the essay offers a fresh perspective on how the built environment can shape the human experience and our collective sense of identity.
